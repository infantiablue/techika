Success is often told as a story of individual effort: someone works hard, makes good decisions, and earns the result. Effort matters, but this story is incomplete. People do not choose every condition in which they learn, take risks, meet collaborators, or receive recognition. Luck shapes the starting point, the timing, and the openings. The better question is not whether luck matters, but how it matters.

A useful distinction is between **pure or passive luck** and **active luck**. Passive luck is received: the circumstances, accidents, and advantages that arrive without a person arranging them. Active luck is cultivated: the unexpected opportunity that becomes more likely because a person has prepared, shown up, experimented, or made their work visible.

## Pure Luck: What We Receive Without Earning

The clearest form of passive luck is being born into wealth. A child born to a wealthy family does not merely inherit money. They may inherit stable housing, good schools, good nutrition, time to study, professional networks, and a safety net if an early choice fails. These advantages shape the range of risks a person can afford to take.

Imagine two equally capable graduates who want to start a business. One can rely on family support while experimenting for a year. The other must immediately earn income to repay debt or support relatives. The first graduate can test an idea, make mistakes, and try again. The second may have to choose stability before the experiment has a chance to mature. If the first person later becomes a successful founder, their work and judgment still deserve credit. But their ability to persist was partly enabled by security they did not create.

This is the consequence of inherited advantage: it reduces the cost of failure. A wealthy person does not necessarily have more courage than anyone else. They may simply be able to survive an error that would seriously damage someone without a financial buffer. The OECD reports that inheritances are unequally distributed, with wealthier households receiving more and higher-value inheritances. The result is not that every wealthy heir succeeds or that everyone without wealth fails. It is that effort begins on an uneven field.

A lottery win is another form of passive luck, perhaps the purest one. A person may choose to buy a ticket, but cannot choose the winning number. A large prize can remove immediate constraints: debt, unstable housing, medical bills, or the need to accept the first available job. It can make space for education, care for family, rest, or a different career.

But lottery wealth also has consequences that money alone cannot solve. It can bring unfamiliar financial decisions, pressure from relatives, public attention, and a sudden change in how other people behave. Research on Swedish lottery players found that larger prizes modestly and persistently reduced labor earnings. This does not mean winners become lazy. It means that an unearned financial cushion can change how much paid work a person needs to do. The prize expands options, but it does not automatically provide judgment, purpose, or healthy boundaries.

Whether inherited wealth or a lottery prize, passive luck changes what is possible. It can give someone time, safety, and a margin for error. It can also produce a misleading story of success if observers mistake an advantage for a personal virtue. Recognizing passive luck should therefore make us more careful when judging who deserves credit for an outcome.

## Active Luck: Being Ready When the Moment Arrives

Active luck is not the power to command chance. It is the practice of becoming ready for chance. It comes from work that creates more opportunities to notice, use, and develop an unexpected opening.

Alexander Fleming’s discovery of penicillin shows how this works. In 1928, mould accidentally contaminated one of Fleming’s bacterial culture plates. Around the mould, he observed that bacteria had been killed. The contamination was passive luck. Fleming did not plan it. In his Nobel banquet speech, he described it as an “accidental contamination” that set him “on another track.”

Yet the accident alone did not create penicillin. Fleming had already spent years as a bacteriologist studying antibacterial substances. Because he had the relevant knowledge, he recognized that the spoiled plate was not merely a failed experiment. He isolated the mould and investigated it. Later, Howard Florey and Ernst Chain built on the work and established its therapeutic value. Fleming had worked long before the fortunate moment arrived. When it did, he was ready to see what others might have thrown away.

Bill Gates offers another example. At Seattle’s Lakeside School, Gates had access to a computer terminal at an unusually early stage of personal computing. He also met Paul Allen there, who later became his Microsoft cofounder. Early access to computing and proximity to a future collaborator were forms of passive luck. Many talented young people had neither.

But Gates and Allen had not been waiting idly for a miracle. They spent substantial time programming, developed practical computing experience, and followed the emerging personal-computer market closely. Their early access gave them an opening; their effort allowed them to use it. The school did not build Microsoft for them. It provided a setting in which years of focused work could turn an unusual opportunity into real capability.

J. K. Rowling provides a third example. On her official site, Rowling says that Harry Potter and Hogwarts came to her “out of nowhere” while she was on a delayed train. The delayed journey was not a strategy. It was an interruption that happened to create the moment in which an idea arrived.

But the train ride did not write the novels. Rowling then spent years imagining, writing, revising, and completing the story. The fortunate moment gave her a beginning, but effort turned that beginning into books. The dramatic origin story is memorable because it is sudden; the work that followed was less visible, but more decisive.

These examples make the meaning of active luck clearer. Fleming could recognize an anomaly because he had trained for years. Gates could act on early computer access because he and Allen had invested serious time in programming. Rowling could develop a passing idea because she had the commitment to turn it into a long piece of work. In each case, luck created an opening, but preparation made the opening useful.

## The Takeaway

The distinction between passive and active luck offers a balanced view of success. Passive luck should make us humble because it reminds us that no one chooses every condition behind an achievement. Active luck should make us purposeful because it shows that uncertainty is not the same as helplessness. We cannot control the world, but we can improve our readiness, expand our relationships, make more attempts, and place ourselves where useful possibilities are more likely to circulate.

This understanding also creates responsibility. People who have benefited from good fortune do not need to feel guilty, but they should avoid treating success as proof of superior worth. A successful manager can make an introduction for a talented newcomer. A business owner can offer an internship to someone without family connections. A teacher can give attention to a student whose ability might otherwise remain hidden. In each case, one person uses their position to create active luck for another.

Luck is neither an excuse for inaction nor a minor detail in achievement. Passive luck shapes the conditions we inherit. Active luck grows from our efforts to encounter more opportunities. Success usually emerges from both: chance creates an opening, action brings us close to it, and preparation allows us to make something of it.

We should therefore work seriously without pretending that work explains everything. We should accept uncertainty without surrendering to it. The wisest approach is to remain humble about the luck we receive and deliberate about the luck we create.
